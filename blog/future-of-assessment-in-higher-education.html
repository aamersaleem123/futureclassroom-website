There's no single fix, but there is a coherent way to think about it — and it starts with recognizing that this isn't really a new problem, just an old one with a faster shortcut. Detection tools are not a strategy AI-detection software is unreliable, produces false positives, and encourages an adversarial relationship between faculty and students. Institutions that lean entirely on detection tools as their integrity strategy are building on unstable ground. Detection can be one input among several — it should never be the whole plan. Redesign assessment, don't just police it The most durable answer is assessment design that makes AI-only submissions genuinely harder to pass off as demonstrating learning. This means favoring in-class components, oral defenses of written work, process documentation (drafts, reflections, iteration logs), and applied tasks tied to specific course content or local context that a generic AI response won't capture well. Separate "using AI" from "outsourcing thinking" Not all AI use in an assignment is dishonest, and treating it as uniformly forbidden usually backfires — students use it anyway, just quietly. Clearer, more useful policy distinguishes between AI as a drafting or research aid (often acceptable, if disclosed) versus AI as a full substitute for the student's own reasoning (not acceptable). This needs to be made explicit per assignment, not left as a vague institutional rule. Build AI literacy into assessment criteria itself Some of the strongest OBE-aligned programs I've reviewed now include AI fluency and critical evaluation of AI output as an explicit learning outcome — teaching students to interrogate and improve on AI-generated work, rather than pretending the tool doesn't exist. This tends to produce more honest classroom conversations than a strict ban ever does. Document your rationale for accreditation purposes Accreditation bodies are increasingly asking institutions how they've adapted assessment practices for the AI era. Having a documented, faculty-approved policy — even an evolving one — puts an institution in a far stronger position during review than an ad hoc, department-by-department approach. The underlying principle Assessment has always been a proxy for learning, and every technology shift has forced a recalibration of that proxy — calculators, the internet, and now generative AI. The institutions that adapt well are the ones that treat this as a normal, manageable evolution in assessment design, grounded in clear outcomes, rather than a crisis to be contained.
