AI readiness needs neither extreme. Here's what actually works. Start with policy, not procurement The instinct is to buy tools first and figure out rules later. This backfires. Before any AI tool reaches a classroom, faculty need clear, written guidance on acceptable use, academic integrity boundaries, and data privacy — the same rigor you'd apply to any other institutional policy. Treat AI literacy as a faculty development priority Institutions that succeed here don't leave faculty to figure things out alone. Structured, hands-on training — not a single seminar, but an ongoing program — makes the difference between AI becoming a genuine teaching asset versus a source of confusion and inconsistent practice across departments. Align with outcome-based education, not against it Many faculty see AI as a threat to OBE and accreditation standards. In my experience, it's the opposite when implemented correctly. AI can strengthen outcome measurement — supporting more consistent rubric application, faster feedback cycles, and better documentation for accreditation evidence — provided the underlying OBE framework is sound to begin with. Build cross-functional governance AI adoption touches teaching, IT, legal/compliance, and academic quality assurance simultaneously. A steering committee that includes all of these voices — not just IT — avoids the common failure mode where a promising pilot stalls because no one considered data governance or accreditation implications early enough. Pilot before you mandate Choose a small number of departments or courses to pilot AI-supported teaching and assessment. Document what works, what doesn't, and where students struggled. Scale only after you have real evidence, not vendor promises. Revisit your statutes and academic regulations Many institutions' governing documents simply don't address AI-assisted work, generative tools in assessment, or related integrity questions. This is unglamorous work, but it's foundational — accreditation bodies increasingly expect institutions to have addressed it explicitly rather than left it ambiguous. The bottom line Universities that treat AI readiness as a governance and capability-building exercise — not just a technology purchase — are the ones that will navigate this transition well. The institutions I've seen struggle are almost always the ones that skipped the policy and training work in favor of moving fast.
