After years of watching faculty adopt (and sometimes misuse) AI tools in higher education, I've found the biggest wins come from small, repeatable tasks rather than dramatic overhauls of how you teach. Here are ten places to begin. 1. Draft lesson plan outlines faster Give ChatGPT your learning outcomes and course level, and ask for a first-draft session outline. You'll still refine it, but you skip the blank-page problem entirely. 2. Generate varied practice questions Ask for multiple versions of the same concept at different difficulty levels. This is especially useful for building differentiated assessments without spending hours writing from scratch. 3. Simplify complex readings for struggling students Paste a dense passage and ask for a plain-language summary at a specific grade level. It's a fast way to create scaffolded versions of the same material. 4. Create rubric language Describe what you're grading and ask for clear, specific rubric criteria. This tends to produce more consistent, defensible language than writing rubrics under time pressure. 5. Draft parent or student communication Turn rough notes into a polished, professional email in seconds — useful for progress updates, meeting summaries, or difficult conversations that need careful wording. 6. Brainstorm real-world examples Ask for examples that connect an abstract concept to students' everyday experience. This consistently improves engagement more than any single teaching technique I've used. 7. Build discussion prompts Request open-ended questions designed to provoke debate rather than single right answers — genuinely useful for seminar-style classes and outcome-based teaching. 8. Get a second opinion on assessment design Describe your assessment and ask where students are likely to struggle or misinterpret instructions. This has caught ambiguous wording in my own exams more than once. 9. Summarize long research papers quickly Useful for staying current in your field, or for preparing digestible summaries for students engaging with primary literature for the first time. 10. Reflect on your own teaching After a class, describe what happened and ask ChatGPT to help you identify patterns or gaps. Treating it as a thinking partner, not just a content generator, is where the real value shows up over a semester. A caution worth repeating None of this replaces professional judgment, and none of it should be handed to students unsupervised without a clear academic integrity conversation first. AI is a multiplier on good teaching practice — it does not substitute for it. Institutions that get this balance right treat AI literacy as a skill to be taught deliberately, not a tool to be banned or ignored.
